## **Latar belakang**

**The Good AI\!** adalah proyek dua tahun (November 2025 – Oktober 2027\) yang membantu media independen, lokal, dan niche di Indonesia menggunakan AI secara bertanggung jawab dalam kerja sehari-hari, dengan fokus pada jurnalisme lingkungan.  
Pada fase pertama riset pengguna, kami bekerja bersama tujuh redaksi kecil dan independen: Bandung Bergerak, Project Multatuli, Mongabay, Ekuatorial, Tirto, Digital Mama, dan AyoBandung. Kami mengikuti perjalanan berita dari perencanaan sampai terbit, lalu berbincang dengan jurnalis, editor, dan pimpinan redaksi tentang di mana beban kerja menumpuk.  
Temuan kami:

* **Cara kerja redaksi mirip.** Ukuran, ritme, dan audiensnya berbeda, tetapi berita selalu melewati tahap yang sama: perencanaan, riset, liputan, penulisan draf, penyuntingan, terbit, distribusi, dan pascaterbit.  
* **AI sudah dipakai, tapi sendiri-sendiri.** Jurnalis memakai alat gratis seperti ChatGPT, Gemini, atau TurboScribe atas inisiatif sendiri, tanpa langganan maupun pedoman dari redaksi.  
* **Lebih cepat belum tentu lebih ringan.** AI mempercepat draf pertama dan transkrip, tetapi kesalahan dan konteks yang hilang memindahkan pekerjaan ke tahap belakang: pengecekan dan penulisan ulang.  
* **Bebannya tidak berhenti di liputan.** Editor dan pimpinan redaksi juga mengurus penggalangan dana, administrasi, dan distribusi, sebagian besar masih secara manual.

Fase 2 beranjak dari memahami masalah menjadi eksplorasi solusi, bersama jurnalis yang mengalami masalah itu sendiri. Ide yang paling kuat dari sesi-sesi ini bisa dikembangkan menjadi purwarupa dan diuji coba di redaksi pada 2027\.

## **Background**

Phase 1 of our user research (P003)1 looked at how small and independent newsrooms work and where AI already enters that work. It covered Bandung Bergerak, Project Multatuli, Mongabay, Ekuatorial, Tirto, Digital Mama and AyoBandung. Despite differences in size, rhythm, and audience, they all move stories through the same stages: plan, research, report, draft, review, publish, distribute, and post-publish.

The research found that AI is already used, but individually and without newsroom support. It also found that AI does not yet reduce total workload. Faster output early in the workflow often moves the effort to verification, rewriting, and editorial review later on.

The research identified recurring pain points across the editorial workflow and the organisation around it. Seven of them can plausibly be addressed with an AI-based solution, and they are the starting point for this sub-project:

> 1. **Information discovery & sense-making.** Information is available but scattered across government portals, company records, reports, regulation, and past coverage, in different formats and from different periods. Before it can support a story, journalists have to check its quality and recency, recover its original context, judge whether it is citable, and connect the pieces themselves.  
> 2. **Source access, safety & emotional judgement.** Whether a source agrees to speak depends on trust, shared language, personal networks, and their past experience with the media. Reaching them can mean remote terrain, bad weather, or repression at demonstrations. In sensitive interviews, journalists balance probing with empathy and protect vulnerable sources, for example by offering a pseudonym.  
> 3. **Transcription inaccuracy.** Noise, overlapping speakers, and mixed languages distort AI transcripts, and even an accurate transcript loses tone, emphasis, and emotion. Journalists replay the recording to correct errors and verify quotes; the transcript helps them find a quote but does not replace the audio.  
> 4. **Interruptions & reporting continuity.** Long-form work runs alongside regular assignments on unrelated topics. Each time they return, journalists reopen their research, interviews, documents, recordings, and notes, recall their earlier reasoning, and recheck what is missing. They lose focus as well as time.  
> 5. **Audience & news distribution.** Each story is reworked into reels, carousels, newsletters, and channel posts: a second, smaller editorial workflow of selecting, compressing, and reframing. Platform algorithms decide the reach, and social media exposure rarely turns into visits to the newsroom's site.  
> 6. **Revenue & content value.** Income is pieced together from grants, memberships, advertising, programmes, and partnerships, each with its own proposals, budgets, contracts, and reports. Meanwhile platforms and AI systems use newsroom content without returning traffic, audience ownership, or compensation (the value capture problem).  
> 7. **Operational & administrative load.** Programme, contributor, partner, funder, and admin work accumulates on editors and newsroom leaders, not on reporters. Invoices, receipts, advances, contributor payments, and membership records come from many people and are collected, checked, and updated by hand.

The research also found an eighth pain point, the lack of AI governance in newsrooms. That one is addressed separately by P009 (AI Code of Conduct Canvas) and is out of scope here.

The research ends with a clear implication: responsible AI should reduce total workload across roles and stages, keep outputs linked to their original sources, stay under human editorial judgement, and be something a small newsroom can govern and maintain. Phase 2 tests that implication by building ideas with journalists rather than for them.

## **Objectives**

The sub-project turns the Phase 1 pain points into **15 lo-fi prototypes** of AI-supported solutions, developed with **45 journalists** across **5 co-creation sessions**.

> 1. **Move from problem to idea.** Help journalists pick a pain point they recognise from their own work and generate AI-supported ideas that address it at the right stage of the workflow.  
> 2. **Make the ideas tangible.** Turn the strongest idea from each team into a lo-fi prototype that a journalist can react to, with an AI developer and a UI/UX designer in the team to speed this up.  
> 3. **Learn what is worth building.** Document each prototype, what journalists thought of it, and the risks it raises, so the most promising ones can be taken forward as AI working models and pilots.

## **Scope and output**

The main output is **15 lo-fi prototypes**, three per session, each documented in a short prototype card and brought together in one final report. We do not arrive with ideas; the teams generate them.

**A lo-fi prototype is:**

> * a rough but concrete representation of an idea: paper sketches, a clickable mock-up, a scripted demo or a simple AI workflow  
> * tied to a specific pain point, a specific user (journalist, editor or newsroom leader) and a specific stage of the editorial workflow  
> * good enough for a journalist to react to: "would I use this, and where would it break?"  
> * designed with a human in the loop, showing where the journalist checks, corrects or decides

**A lo-fi prototype is not:**

> * production software, a hosted service or a finished product  
> * a commitment to build: selecting what moves forward happens after Phase 2  
> * a tool selection exercise or AI training programme  
> * a place for real source material: prototypes use dummy or public data only

**Each prototype card records:** the pain point and who has it; the workflow stage; the idea in one sentence; how AI is used and where the human stays in control; the sketch or demo; journalists' feedback; risks (accuracy, data and source safety, workload shift); and what to test next.

**Supporting outputs:** a session summary for each of the 5 sessions, a pain point coverage tracker, and a final Ideation & Lo-fi Prototype report that brings together all 15 prototypes and recommends a shortlist for M3.2.

## **Approach and methodology**

The work runs as 5 co-creation sessions. Sessions are **not themed**: in each session, every team chooses the pain point it wants to work on. What is learned in one session shapes the next. All dates below are tentative.

### **Preparation (before session 1\)**

> * **Materials:** the lead facilitator turns the Phase 1 findings into session materials: a one-page brief per pain point with quotes and workflow stage, the personas, and a prototype card template.  
> * **Team briefing:** AI developers and UI/UX designers are briefed on the research and on lo-fi prototyping methods suited to a one-day session.  
> * **Recruitment:** 9 journalists are confirmed for each session, with a mix of roles (e.g. data journalists, environmental journalists, editors and media owners).

### **Co-creation sessions (×5)**

**Question:** Which AI-supported solution would actually reduce this pain point for this newsroom role, and what would it look like?

> * **Team shape:** each session has 3 teams. Each team has 3 journalists, 1 full-stack AI developer, 1 UI/UX designer and 1 co-facilitator, and produces 1 prototype.  
> * **Choosing a problem:** each team picks one of the seven pain points, based on what its journalists experience most. Teams in the same session may pick the same pain point if they approach it differently.  
> * **Flow:** understand the pain point → generate ideas → choose one → build the lo-fi prototype → test it with journalists from the other teams → capture feedback on the prototype card. The detailed session design is set by the lead facilitator (see Session logistics and agenda).  
> * **Output per session:** 3 lo-fi prototypes with their cards, and a session summary.

### **Coverage across sessions**

Because teams choose freely, some pain points may be chosen often and others not at all. The lead facilitator keeps a **pain point coverage tracker** after each session and adjusts the next one. If a pain point has not been taken by the end of session 4, it becomes a priority in session 5\. For example, if no team has worked on audience & distribution after four sessions, session 5 invites journalists with distribution experience and asks at least one team to take it on.

### **Synthesis and report (after session 5\)**

> * Bring all 15 prototype cards together and compare them by pain point, workflow stage, user and feasibility.  
> * Identify patterns: which pain points attracted the most ideas, where AI helped, and where journalists rejected it.  
> * Recommend a shortlist of prototypes to take forward as AI working models (M3.2a) and pilots (M3.2b).

## **Principles, consent, and confidentiality**

> * **Human in the loop.** Every prototype shows where the journalist checks, corrects, or decides. AI supports; journalists make the final call.  
> * **Built with journalists, not for them.** Teams choose the problems and shape the ideas. No solution is fixed in advance.  
> * **Do no harm.** No real source material, personal data, or unpublished reporting goes into prototypes or AI tools. Teams use dummy or public data.  
> * **Informed consent.** Sessions are recorded only with consent. Participants can go off the record at any time.  
> * **Credit.** Participating journalists and partners are credited as co-creators of the prototypes, unless they ask not to be.  
> * **Open output.** Prototype cards and the final report are intended to be shared openly within the project \[licence TBC\].

## *Version 1· 10 October 2026 · The Good AI\! (P8509) · Sub-project P012*

## **Background**

**The Good AI\!** is a two-year project (November 2025 – October 2027\) that helps independent, local, niche media in Indonesia use AI responsibly in their daily work, with a focus on environmental journalism.

In the first phase of our user research, we worked with seven small and independent newsrooms: Bandung Bergerak, Project Multatuli, Mongabay, Ekuatorial, Tirto, Digital Mama, and AyoBandung. We followed how stories move from planning to publication, and talked with journalists, editors, and newsroom leaders about where the work piles up.

What we found:

* **Newsrooms work in similar ways.** Despite differences in size, rhythm, and audience, stories move through the same stages: plan, research, report, draft, review, publish, distribute, and post-publish.  
* **AI is already used, but alone.** Journalists use free tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, or Turboscribe on their own initiative, without newsroom subscriptions, guidelines, or support.  
* **Faster is not lighter.** AI speeds up first drafts and transcripts, but errors and missing context move the work to checking, rewriting, and editorial review later on.  
* **The load goes beyond reporting.** Editors and newsroom leaders also carry fundraising, administration, and distribution work, much of it by hand.

Phase 2 moves from understanding the problems to trying out solutions, together with the journalists who live with them. The ideas that hold up best in these sessions may be developed further into working tools and piloted in newsrooms in 2027\.

## **Why you are invited**

You are one of 9 journalists invited to a one-day co-creation session. It is one of 5 sessions in this series, with 45 journalists taking part in total. In each session, small teams of journalists, AI developers, and designers turn real newsroom problems into quick, rough prototypes of AI-supported tools.

The research identified seven recurring problems where AI might genuinely help. In this session, we ask which AI-supported ideas are actually worth building, and only journalists can tell us that.

## **The problems we will work on**

Your team chooses one of these problems, based on what you experience most in your own work:

1. **Information discovery & sense-making:** the information exists but is scattered across many sources and formats, so journalists have to validate it, find its original context, and connect it themselves before it can support a story  
2. **Source access, safety & emotional judgement:** reaching sources depends on trust, language, and difficult terrain, and sensitive interviews mean protecting sources without causing them harm  
3. **Transcription inaccuracy:** noisy field audio and mixed languages make AI transcripts wrong, and the source's tone and emotion are lost, so journalists still go back to the recording  
4. **Interruptions & reporting continuity:** long-form reporting is often cut off by routine assignments, so each time journalists come back they have to reopen their materials and rebuild the context from scratch  
5. **Audience & news distribution:** one story has to be reworked for every platform (reels, carousels, newsletters), yet social media reach rarely brings readers back to the newsroom's site  
6. **Revenue & content value:** newsroom income is pieced together from grants, memberships, ads, and programmes, each adding its own paperwork, while platforms and AI use newsroom content without anything coming back  
7. **Operational & administrative load:** invoices, receipts, contributor payments, and programme reports are still collected and checked by hand, and the load piles up on editors and newsroom leaders

## **What we will make together**

* **Rough prototypes, not finished products.** Each team makes one lo-fi prototype: a sketch, a clickable mock-up or a simple demo, good enough to react to.  
* **Built around your experience.** You bring the problem and judge the ideas. The AI developer, UI/UX designer and facilitator in your team help make the idea tangible quickly.  
* **Humans stay in charge.** Every prototype has to show where the journalist checks, corrects or decides.  
* **Credited and open.** Each prototype is documented on a prototype card and shared within the project. You are credited as a co-creator unless you prefer not to be.
